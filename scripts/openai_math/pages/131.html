The result in plain terms

A network analyst who finds many triangles in a social network, or a recurring wiring pattern in a gene network, has to ask whether the finding is surprising. The standard comparison is against random networks in which every node keeps exactly the number of connections it has in the real one. If such random networks show the same pattern, the pattern follows from the degrees alone.

The usual way to produce such a network is edge swapping. Start from the real network. Pick two edges, a–b and c–d, and replace them by a–c and b–d, unless that would duplicate an existing edge or join a node to itself. Every node keeps its degree. Repeat many times. This is double_edge_swap in NetworkX and degree-preserving rewire in igraph.

The method rests on a hope: that after a reasonable number of swaps the network is a fair draw from all networks with those degrees, each equally likely. The process could instead stay for an astronomically long time in one corner of the possibilities, and the comparison would be biased with no outward sign. The theorem rules that out. For every list of degrees that some simple network can have, the swap process is within a fixed distance of the uniform distribution after at most $2n^8$ steps, where $n$ is the number of nodes.

What was known

Kannan, Tetali and Vempala posed the question in 1999. Cooper, Dyer and Greenhill proved polynomial mixing in 2007 when every node has the same degree. Later work by Greenhill and Sfragara, and by Erdős, Greenhill, Mezei, Miklós, Soltész and Soukup, extended this to families of degree sequences satisfying a stability condition. A degree sequence outside those families had no guarantee.

Why it is usable today

The algorithm is the one already installed. The theorem adds the missing statement about it: swap long enough and the sample is uniform, with no condition on the degrees to check first. An analyst no longer has to ask whether a particular network falls inside a proved class.

The manuscript also gives a sampler whose output is exactly uniform, with expected polynomial running time, for cases where an approximate draw is not acceptable.

What it does not give

The bound $2n^8$ is not a stopping rule. For a thousand nodes it is $2\times10^{24}$ steps. Practice runs a small multiple of the number of edges, often ten to a hundred swaps per edge, and the theorem does not certify that budget. It certifies that the process has no hidden exponential trap.

The proof analyses a version that proposes four nodes at random, where toolkits propose two edges. Both have the uniform distribution as their limit; the speeds differ. The theorem covers simple undirected networks. The directed and bipartite forms of the conjecture are separate statements.
