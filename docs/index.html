Pure mathematics has never once been useless.

Twelve branches of it were declared useless, by name, in writing. Here is what happened to each one.

For two centuries, the same claim has been made about whatever branch of mathematics looked furthest from any machine: this is beautiful, and it will never be used for anything. Sometimes a specific person said so, on the record, in a specific year. Sometimes nobody bothered to say it — the field was simply left alone, unfunded and unread, because everyone assumed it obviously had no use. Either way, the claim was falsifiable. This site checks it, case by case, against dates, patents, and DOIs rather than vibes.

The founding document of the myth is G. H. Hardy's A Mathematician's Apology (1940), which states three times that number theory has no practical or military value — and, for good measure, that relativity doesn't either. Hardy's own two examples were both wrong within a lifetime: relativity became the correction GPS satellites need every day to stay accurate to within meters, and number theory became the padlock icon in every browser. The Introduction tells that story in full; the Ledger tells the other eleven.

The pattern

Twelve times, independently, in fields that never spoke to each other.

What counts as “declared useless”

Some of these are a named person, on the record, saying the field was pointless — Hardy on number theory, Lord Kelvin on quaternions and, separately, on knot theory, Gauss privately fearing to publish non-Euclidean geometry, Burnside doubting representation theory would ever produce another result. Others are worse than a dismissal: nobody thought the field was even worth criticizing. Boolean algebra, point-set topology, and Hilbert space theory sat for decades with no recorded critic and no recorded champion, because no one could imagine a use for them either way. Both patterns count, and each case on the Ledger is tagged with which one applies, and how well the quote or claim is sourced — nothing here is asserted that couldn't be pinned to a book, a paper, or a primary document.
